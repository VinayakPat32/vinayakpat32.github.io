F: force

Measured in newtons (N). The restoring force the spring exerts.

x: extension

Measured in metres (m). How far the spring is stretched or compressed from its natural length.

What Is Hooke's Law?

Stretch a rubber band and the harder you pull, the harder it pulls back. Hooke's Law says the same about springs: the force needed to stretch or compress a spring is directly proportional to the change in its length, as long as you stay within its elastic limit.

In symbols: F ∝ x, which becomes F = kx once you add the constant of proportionality k.

Robert Hooke published this in 1676 as a Latin anagram, later decoded as "ut tensio, sic vis", meaning "as the extension, so the force."

Why the negative sign?

In textbooks the law is written F = −kx. Pull the spring right (+x) and it pulls back left (−F). Push it left (−x) and it pushes back right (+F). That is a restoring force. In Class 11 numerical problems you only need magnitudes, so you write F = kx and treat every value as positive. This calculator works the same way.

Elastic limit: Stretch a spring too far and it will not return to its original length. Past this point the force–extension graph is no longer a straight line and Hooke's Law stops working. Engineers always keep springs well inside this limit.

Spring Constant (k) and Spring Constant with Mass

The spring constant tells you how stiff a spring is. A slinky has k of about 1 to 2 N/m, a mattress coil about 800 to 1,500 N/m, and a car suspension spring about 15,000 to 35,000 N/m.

If you hang a mass m from a spring and it extends by x, the spring force equals the weight:

k = mg ÷ x
g = 9.8 m/s²

Worked example: spring constant from a hanging mass

A 500 g mass (0.5 kg) stretches a spring by 10 cm (0.1 m). Find k.

F = mg = 0.5 × 9.8 = 4.9 N, then k = F ÷ x = 4.9 ÷ 0.1

k = 49 N/m

Hooke's Law Calculation Examples

Always convert centimetres to metres before you substitute.

Example 1: find force (F = kx)

A spring with k = 300 N/m is compressed by 8 cm. What force does it exert?

x = 0.08 m, so F = 300 × 0.08

F = 24 N

Example 2: find spring constant (k = F/x)

A 15 N force stretches a spring by 5 cm. What is k?

x = 0.05 m, so k = 15 ÷ 0.05

k = 300 N/m

Example 3: find extension (x = F/k)

A spring with k = 500 N/m has a 25 N force applied. How far does it stretch?

x = 25 ÷ 500

x = 0.05 m = 5 cm

Example 4: bungee cord

A cord with k = 100 N/m is stretched 8 m by a 70 kg jumper. What is the spring force at the lowest point?

F = 100 × 8 = 800 N, and the jumper's weight is 70 × 9.8 = 686 N.

F = 800 N, which is greater than the weight, so the cord pulls the jumper back up.

Hooke's Law as Stress and Strain

Hooke's Law covers any elastic material, not just springs. For a metal rod or a bone you use stress and strain instead of force and extension.

Stress (σ)

σ = F / A. Force per unit area, in pascals (N/m²).

Strain (ε)

ε = ΔL / L₀. Extension divided by original length. It has no unit.

Young's modulus (E)

E = σ / ε. A property of the material itself, in Pa or GPa.

The link: for a spring F ∝ x. For a material, stress ∝ strain, and the constant of proportionality is Young's modulus.

Hooke's Law in Real Life

Anything that stretches, compresses or bends and then returns to its shape is using Hooke's Law: pen clickers, mattress coils, spring balances, trampolines, car suspensions, aircraft landing gear, orthodontic wires, bridge cables and even the cantilever in an atomic force microscope. The equation stays F = kx. Only the size of k changes.

Stored elastic energy follows the same idea: E = ½kx². It explains why a drawn bow launches an arrow and why a door closer shuts gently.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the SI unit of spring constant (k)?

Newton per metre (N/m). It is the force needed to stretch or compress the spring by one metre.

Does Hooke's Law apply to compression as well as stretching?

Yes. Within the elastic limit, F = kx holds for both extension and compression, where x is the size of the change in length.

How do I calculate spring constant with mass?

Use k = mg / x. A 1 kg mass that stretches a spring by 0.2 m gives k = (1 × 9.8) / 0.2 = 49 N/m.

What is the difference between spring constant and Young's modulus?

Spring constant belongs to a specific spring and depends on its coil size, wire thickness and number of coils. Young's modulus is a property of the material itself, so steel is about 200 GPa whether it is a wire or a rod.

What happens when springs are in series or parallel?

In series, 1/ktotal = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂, so the combination is softer. In parallel, ktotal = k₁ + k₂, so it is stiffer.

Why is there a negative sign in F = −kx?

The spring's restoring force acts opposite to the displacement. For magnitude problems you can use F = kx.
